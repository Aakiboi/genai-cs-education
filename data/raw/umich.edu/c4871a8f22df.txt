Course Overview
This course explores the intersection of AI and systems with a focus on how AI
techniques can help design, optimize, and manage computer systems. Modern
systems are facing growing complexity that arises from scale, heterogeneous
hardware, deep dependencies, and highly dynamic workloads. These complexities
make it difficult to rely on traditional approaches to reason about, control,
and predict the system behavior. At the same time, as the backbone of computing
services, systems today face ever more stringent requirements about their
reliability, availability, performance, security, etc. The advances of AI
techniques provide opportunities to address these gaps.
The course covers a broad range of systems, including operating systems,
distributed systems, databases, storage, and networking. It examines how AI
techniques can be applied to key system components such as schedulers, file
systems, caching and memory management, query optimizers, and distributed
protocols. Rather than focusing solely on parameter tuning, the course will
take a holistic view of how learning-based approaches can deeply integrate with
complex systems to guide system design, implementation, policy decisions, and
augment or rethink traditional system mechanisms.
The course traces the evolution of AI for systems research. It begins with
early work that predates deep learning, such as statistical modeling and
classical machine learning for system optimization. It then progresses to more
recent advances, such as learned system components and leveraging large
language models (LLMs) for system-related tasks.
Scope
: This course focuses on
AI for Systems
: using AI techniques to design, optimize, and manage
computer systems. It is
NOT
about
Systems for AI
, which studies
systems and infrastructure for supporting AI workloads.
Learning Objectives
By the end of the course, students will be able to:
Understand systems problems that are suitable for AI and machine learning techniques.
Gain insights on how learning techniques integrate with conventional system mechanisms, including their trade-offs.
Critique current AI-for-systems research, including its assumptions, methodology, costs, and limitations.
Identify open problems at the intersection of AI and computer systems.
Design and evaluate new learning-enabled system components.

machine learning (e.g., EECS 445) is helpful but not required.

Course Structure
This course is research oriented. It is structured as a mixture of
instructor-led lectures, student-led paper presentations, guest lectures and
discussions. There are very few traditional lectures; most class meetings are
discussions of research papers that everyone is expected to have read
beforehand.
Readings
This course does not have a required textbook. The course readings consist
primarily of research papers from top systems and interdisciplinary venues such
as
SOSP, OSDI, NSDI, SIGCOMM, SIGMOD/VLDB, ASPLOS,
and
MLSys
. These papers
include both foundational work and recent progress so students understand the
evolution of ideas and recurring challenges.
Most classes will have two
required
papers to read. You
MUST
read the required papers
before
class and be prepared to
discuss them. For some topics, we will list additional optional papers. You are
encouraged, but not required, to read those. Students often find it useful to
form a reading group to discuss papers together before the class period, and we
encourage the practice.

Structure and Policies
Participation
: Participation
grades are based on consistent engagement with course discussions and
activities rather than speaking frequency alone. Students are evaluated using
multiple indicators, including:
demonstrated preparation for weekly readings;
contributions to discussion that advance technical understanding;
asking thoughtful questions or offering constructive critiques; and
providing feedback during peer presentations.
Each student will also serve as a discussion leader for assigned papers during
the semester, which contributes to the participation component. If you remain
silent in the class, you will gain little out of this course.
Paper Review
: You are required to
submit a brief review for the required reading in each class. Reviews
must be turned in
by 12 pm
on the day of
the class. Please refer to the detailed requirements on
this page
.
Presentation
: Each student will
be responsible for presenting and leading discussion for one or more research
papers during the semester. The goal of the presentation is not only to
summarize the paper, but also to critically analyze its motivation, design
choices, methodology, and implications. Presenters are expected to discuss with
the instructor in advance to ensure appropriate depth and scope. Detailed
requirements are described on
this page
.
Attendance
:
Three
absences of
classes are allowed for good reasons (e.g., conference presentation,
interview). However, reviews of the papers must still be turned in.
Discussion
: You are strongly
encouraged to discuss the papers with other students in the class – you may
have insights that others do not, and vice versa. Often students form reading
groups, which I heartily encourage. Note that group discussion, however, is not
an effective substitute for actually reading the paper.
Project
: A major component of
this course is a semester-long, research-oriented project on using AI
techniques to address a systems problem. You will work in a team of 2-3
students. Detailed requirements for the project are described on
this page
.
