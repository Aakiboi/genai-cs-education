4+1 MSCS (Five-Year) Undergrad Program
The new CS 4+1 MS program offers an opportunity for Duke undergraduates to earn an MS degree in CS (MSCS) with an extra (fifth) year at Duke. The MSCS degree is in addition to your undergraduate degree. This 4+1 program may be a good path for you if you are able to take two or more “extra” graduate courses as an undergraduate, beyond any courses you need for your undergraduate degree. The 4+1 MSCS program is suitable primarily for CS majors and CS minors.

CS Undergraduate Course Policy & Requirements
S/U Course Credit
MATH 111, MATH 112 and COMPSCI 101 -- Only these three courses are accepted as S/U (if they can be taken S/U) and count towards degree requirements for CompSci majors and minors. For other majors and minors you plan to have, you need to check if they will also allow these courses to count if taken S/U.
For IDM majors, only these three courses -- MATH 111, MATH 112 and COMPSCI 101 -- are accepted as S/U (if they can be taken S/U) and count towards the CompSci part of your IDM. Check with your other major in your IDM to find out whether they will also allow these courses to count if taken S/U.
It is possible these courses may not be offered with the option to take them S/U.

HOUSECS 59-02 | Neetcode 75 : Algorithmic Thinking for Software Engineering
Instructors:
Benita Besa (
bcb63@duke.edu
) and Raphael Mukondiwa (
rtm45@duke.edu
)
Faculty Sponsor:
Dr. Susan Rodger
Monday 5:15PM - 6:45PM  -
Perkins Link 059 (Seminar 1)
Technical interviews are a gateway to software engineering careers, yet many students struggle to develop the pattern recognition and problem-solving intuition needed to succeed. This course demystifies the coding interview by teaching algorithmic thinking through the NeetCode 75, a curated collection of essential problems that cover fundamental data structures and algorithms. Rather than memorizing solutions, students will learn to recognize patterns, break down complex problems systematically, and build confidence in their technical abilities. Through hands-on problem-solving sessions and collaborative learning, we'll explore core concepts like arrays, hashing, linked lists, trees, graphs, dynamic programming, and more. Whether you're preparing for internship interviews or simply want to strengthen your algorithmic foundation, this course provides practical skills that translate directly to real-world software engineering and technical assessment success.

Special Topics in Building Agents: From Reasoning and Tools to Learning and Evolution | Instructor: Jian Pei / AI is rapidly moving beyond models that generate content toward agents that reason, plan, remember, use tools, take actions, collaborate, learn from experience, and improve over time. This course introduces the principles and technologies for building intelligent agents on top of modern foundation models, with an emphasis on real-world, data-rich applications. We will study reasoning and planning; memory, RAG, and context engineering; world models; multimodal perception; tool and function calling; Model Context Protocol (MCP); coding and computer-use agents; agent runtimes and orchestration; and multi-agent systems. A central theme is the interplay between data and agents: how agents acquire, integrate, evaluate, and learn from data and how they enable more intelligent and increasingly autonomous data science. These ideas naturally extend to AI for science and discovery, where agents can connect data, models, tools, experiments, and feedback to help close the discovery loop. The course also explores how agents learn from experience, self-correct, acquire skills, collaborate, self-improve, and evolve, together with evaluation, reliability, security, safety, and human oversight. It is self-contained, technically grounded, and application-oriented, connecting foundational ideas with modern agent technologies and real-world systems. Prerequisites: Linear algebra, multivariable calculus, and probability. COMPSCI 270

COMPSCI 390-03
- Distributed Systems | Instructor: Jeffrey Chase / This course introduces core concepts in distributed systems with supporting elements from networking, operating systems, and cryptosystems.   Examples are drawn from real-world platforms for cloud computing, Internet/Web, and the software supply chain.  Networked services: transport streams, request/response messaging, cloud instances, elastic scaling.   Secure naming and identity: domain names, identity services, authentication, and certified trust.   Reliable data at scale: sharding and replication, snapshots, updates and version histories, consistency, coordination, recovery, and consensus.  As time allows we draw out connections to cyber-policy topics such as cryptocurrency and cyberwar. Prerequisite: COMPSCI 210 or COMPSCI 250D.

COMPSCI 590-03
- Causal Inference, Fairness, and Explanations in Data Analysis | Instructor: Sudeepa Roy / As commonly known as “Correlation is not Causation”, the problem of causal inference goes beyond correlation, association, or model-based prediction analysis, and is indispensable in health, social sciences, and other domains. Causal inference lays the foundation of sound, robust policy making by providing a means to estimate the impact of a certain “intervention” to the world. While the gold standard of causal inference is performing randomized experiments, often they are not possible due to ethical/cost reasons. Hence in practice, observational study under certain assumptions is used. A dataset can tell us very different stories based on how we look at it (e.g., Simpson Paradox), so it is also important to understand the right way to look at a dataset, e.g., what variables to condition on for any (causal) conclusions in data analysis. We will discuss two causal models: Graphical Causal Model (Pearl) from AI, and Potential Outcome Framework (Rubin) from Statistics. We will also discuss applications of causal analysis to Fairness and explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI), which aim to address the growing concerns about the complexity and opacity of data-driven decision-making systems for important applications. This course will have a mix of lectures and presentation of research papers. The grade will be given based on class participation (reading/critiquing papers), presenting and leading discussions on 1-2 papers, a few assignments, and a group class project (no exams). Prerequisites for Undergraduate students:
COMPSCI 201 and COMPSCI 230
AND
one of the following: COMPSCI 330 or COMPSCI 371 or COMPSCI 526/CBB 526/ECE 583 or COMPSCI 531D or COMPSCI 532 or COMPSCI 570
COMPSCI 590-04
- Special Topics in Large Language Models | Instructor: Bhuwan Dhingra / Seminar course on recent advances in large language models, with an emphasis on detecting AI-generated and mixed human–AI content. The course will provide an overview of how LLMs are developed, trained, and evaluated. At least half of the course will focus on detection techniques, their limitations, and their evaluation under realistic conditions, including AI-assisted editing and human revision of AI-generated content. Students are expected to read and present research papers and complete project-style assignments. Topics include:
- Transformers and language modeling
- Pretraining and data curation
- Post-training and reinforcement learning
- Statistical and classifier-based AI detection
- Watermarking and content provenance
- Detection of mixed human–AI content
- Detection across text, code, and multimodal content
- Robustness and adversarial attacks
Prerequisites: COMPSCI 371 OR COMPSCI 372 OR COMPSCI 375 OR COMPSCI 575 OR EQUIVALENT
COMPSCI 590-05
- Special Topics in Machine Learning for Healthcare | Instructor: Monica Agrawal / This course will introduce students to machine learning in healthcare. The course will involve learning how health data is generated, ML techniques for modeling health data, and the considerations that arise during evaluation and deployment. There will be a particular focus on addressing the unique technical challenges that arise in AI for medicine, due to issues such as data missingness, confounding, noisy labels, and distribution shift.
Familiarity with deep learning, probability, and Python programming expected. No healthcare or biology background required. Undergraduate students should have taken an AI or ML core course.
COMPSCI 590-06
- Interpretable Machine Learning | Instructor: Cynthia Rudin / Topics may include: sparse models for tabular data and optimization of them (including decision trees, generalized additive models, risk scores and other model classes), Rashomon sets, interpretable neural networks for computer vision, theoretical foundations of interpretable machine learning including the Rashomon set theory, dimension reduction for data visualization, interpretable matching for causal inference, and applications. Prerequisite for Undergraduate and Graduate students: COMPSCI 671 or COMPSCI 672.
COMPSCI 590-07
- Seminar in Computational Biology: Protein Structure & Drug Design using CS, AI, & ML | Instructor: Bruce Donald / The Nobel Prize was given in 2025 for AI-enabled protein structure prediction and design. This year's seminar will focus on AI, Machine Learning, and algorithmic approaches to computational and experimental studies of protein structure and design. However, background material and fundamental basic science, algorithms, and computational methods will be covered in order to get to the foothills of this field. Some of the most challenging and influential opportunities for Physical Geometric Algorithms (PGA) arise in developing and applying information technology to understand the molecular machinery of the cell. Recent work shows that PGA techniques may be fruitfully applied to the challenges of structural molecular biology and rational drug design. Concomitantly, a wealth of interesting computational problems arise in proposed methods for discovering new pharmaceuticals. This seminar course focuses on topics in computational biology. We will emphasize themes that unite algorithms, modelling, machine learning, or experimental results. Topics will include algorithms, modeling, machine learning, and experimental validation for several areas, including protein design, protein:protein interactions, structural biology, structural immunology, and structure-based drug design. For those who have taken a class with me previously, this semester we will read different papers, so please feel free to sign up. Textbook: Algorithms in Structural Molecular Biology, MIT Press (Cambridge: 2011). Prerequisite for Undergraduate students: COMPSCI 231 or COMPSCI 330.
If you have only had COMPSCI 230 (or a substitute), then you must have taken COMPSCI 330 first.
If you have only had COMPSCI 231,  then you may take this course without COMPSCI 330 or concurrently with COMPSCI 330.
COMPSCI 590-08
- Generative Models: Foundations and Applications | Instructor: Anru Zhang / This course provides an in-depth exploration of the mathematical foundations and real-world applications of generative models, focusing on healthcare data. Students will learn key concepts of state-of-the-art techniques such as variational autoencoders, generative adversarial networks, diffusion models, flow matching. The course covers theory and practice, with applications ranging from synthetic data generation to fairness improvement in machine learning tasks. Prerequisites for Undergraduate students: COMPSCI 201 AND (MATH 230 or MATH 231 or MATH 240 or MATH 340) AND (MATH 216 or MATH 218 or MATH 221).

- Programming Language Concepts | Instructor: Danfeng Zhang / This course equips students with the knowledge to not only use programming languages proficiently but also to understand why they work the way they do and how good language designs help programmers to write reliable code. COMPSCI 375
- Natural Language Processing | Instructor: Dhingra / This new course will count as a general elective for the CompSci BS and AB degree requirements or as an elective for the AI/ML concentration. This is an introductory course in natural language processing, covering core computational problems involving human language, and both classical techniques such as bag-of-words, n-grams and and conditional random fields, as well as modern neural approaches such as word vectors, recurrent neural networks and transformers. We will also lay the foundation for understanding large language models and their scaling properties. Focus will be on key NLP tasks such as text classification, language modeling, machine translation and information retrieval. Prerequisite: COMPSCI 201 and familiarity with Python. It is recommended, though not required, to also have completed one of COMPSCI 270 or COMPSCI 371 or COMPSCI 372.

COMPSCI 552
- Programming Languages | Instructor: Danfeng Zhang /  Whether designing systems, writing high-quality code or learning a new programming language, the ability to reason about language behavior and structure is a critical skill. Through analytical comparison, the course examines core topics such as language syntax and semantics, type systems, control structures, memory management, as well as cutting-edge language features, such as formal verification and static program analysis. Emphasis will be placed on both theoretical foundations and practical insights. By the end of the course, students will be able to critically evaluate language design choices, understand the trade-offs involved in language implementation, and apply this knowledge to write clearer, more reliable programs.
