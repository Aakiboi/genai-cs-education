Computer Science Variable Title Courses
Beginning in Fall 2021, Computer Science graduate level variable title courses will be offered as described below.  CS graduate seminars allow students to explore new and cutting edge topics that are not part of the regular curriculum. Students are encouraged to broaden their knowledge via these courses. The guidelines regarding inclusion on your formal Graduate School Plan of Study are below.
CS 49000 (Traditional Course) - will be used for topics not currently covered in the CS undergraduate curriculum. Each offering follows a traditional course structure with textbook(s), assignments, exams, and week-by-week content synopsis described in a syllabus.
Some CS 49000 courses
MAY
be approved to be used as an elective for a track. See below to check if a course has been approved for track use.
CS 49000* (Individual Study Course) - will be used exclusively for Individual Study offerings.
See step 7 in the
CS Grad Student Registration
for details on how to register for individual study courses
Step 7 (Individual Study Courses) is the same process for graduate students and undergraduate students.

one-page course description approved by the CS faculty instructor

CS Grad Student Registration

CS 59200 (Seminar Course) - is a course that deviates from a traditional course structure by emphasizing student presentations and projects over lectures, assignments, and exams.
Course offerings are reviewed each semester to determine if they will be permitted on a PhD plan of study or as an MS plan elective

CS 49000AISS: AI Safety And Security
Instructors: Bertino, Elisa and Celik, Berkay
Days/Time: W 5:30p-8:20p
Location: HAAS G066
CS TRACK APPROVAL: Elective for Security and Machine Intelligence tracks
Pre-requisites:C or better CS 37300 or CS 47100
Course description: This course introduces the safety and security of modern artificial intelligence (AI) systems, including machine learning models, foundation models, large language models (LLMs), retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and AI agents. The course examines how vulnerabilities and unsafe behaviors can arise throughout the AI lifecycle, from training data and model development to deployment and interaction with external systems. Students will learn how to construct threat models for AI systems and study representative attacks, including adversarial examples, data poisoning and backdoors, model extraction and privacy attacks, prompt injection, jailbreaks, and attacks against AI agents.

CS 49000IQC: Intro To Quantum Computing
Instructors: Peng, Yuxiang and Zhan, Wei
Days/Time: MWF 10:30a-11:20a
Location: HAAS G066
CS TRACK APPROVAL: NONE
Pre-requisites: C or better in CS 18200 and MA 26500 or MA 35100
Course description: This course introduces the foundations of quantum computing from a computer science perspective. The course begins with a review of mathematical tools needed for quantum computation, including complex vector spaces, linear operators, and tensor products. Students will learn quantum states, superposition, measurement, and entanglement, and study how quantum gates and circuits process quantum information. The course introduces representative quantum algorithms, including Deutsch–Jozsa, Bernstein–Vazirani, Grover’s search, and Shor’s factoring algorithm. Emphasis is placed on their central ideas and sources of computational advantage rather than advanced mathematical proofs. Students will also construct and simulate circuits using Qiskit and learn basic quantum error correction and practical challenges of quantum computing. Through theoretical homework, programming assignments, and a final group literature-review presentation, students will analyze quantum computations and explore selected directions in the field.

Instructor: Spafford, Eugene
Days/Time: T/TH 3:00p-4:15p
Location: LWSN B134
OK MS as elective: YES
OK PhD as 1 of 6: PENDING
Pre-requisites: UG students B or better in CS 42600. GR students CS 52600
Course description: This seminar is a map of the standards, regulations, and governance structures that shape cybersecurity, privacy, and AI-risk programs in government and regulated industry. Students read the primary documents themselves, in short excerpts: the NIST Risk Management Framework and Cybersecurity Framework, SP 800-53 and SP 800-171, ISO/IEC 27001, the CIS Controls, HIPAA, PCI DSS, CMMC, FedRAMP, GDPR, NIS2, DORA, the Cyber Resilience Act, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001, and the EU AI Act. For each, the questions are the same: what is it, who issues it, who must follow it and why, how is it structured, what does it require, how does it relate to the others, and where does one go to learn more.
CS 59200FPM: Frontier/Proof Mechanization
Instructor: Rompf, Tiark
Days/Time: T/TH 10:30a-11:45a
Location: BRNG 1238
OK MS as elective: YES
OK PhD as 1 of 6: YES
Pre-requisites: UG students need instructor permission
Course description: Fueled by AI, proof mechanization has gone from a niche topic to the cusp of widespread adoption in software verification and general mathematics. This course will explore advanced topics at the frontier of proof mechanization foundations, techniques, and applications. The range of topics is open-ended and will largely be driven by student interest, but we expect to cover topics ranging from the foundational theory of dependent types and the "propositions as types, programs as proofs" paradigm to implementation techniques of proof assistants themselves and to advanced proof techniques for challenging mathematical structures. The course will be research-oriented and hands-on: we will read and discuss original papers from the forefront of research, and participants will propose and complete a course project, consisting of a meaningful proof mechanization effort.

CS 59300ASl: Architecture Security
Instructor: Taram, Kazem
Days/Time: T/TH 3:00p-4:15p
Location: GRIS 133
OK MS as elective: YES
OK PhD as 1 of 6: YES
Pre-requisites: UG students C or better in CS 25000
Course description:This course will focus on the interaction of computer security and computer architecture. We will start with the basic architectural background: caches and memory hierarchy, out-of-order execution, speculative execution, and branch prediction. Then we will discuss fundamental system security concepts such as isolation, memory safety, side- and covert-channels, etc. This gives us the necessary background to dive deeper into understanding of how microarchitecture can be exploited to leak information. We will discuss the classic and recent attacks that leverage different architectural structures/components which include caches, branch prediction, DVFS, DRAM, and accelerators/FPGAs. We will then cover high-performance architectural solutions to mitigate those attacks. We will also talk about fundamentally secure approaches and architectures such as constant-time programming, capability-based architectures, and information flow tracking. Finally, we will discuss how architecture can provide efficient support for security and privacy, where we talk about solutions such as Trusted-Execution Enviourments (TEEs) and architecture support for memory safety and isolation.
CS 59300HRI: Human-Robot Interaction
Instructor: Jeong, Sooyeon
Days/Time: T/TH 9:00a-10:15a
Location: LWSN 1106
OK MS as elective: YES
OK PhD as 1 of 6: YES
Pre-requisites: none
Course description: Robots are making their way into our world and will soon interact with people in our everyday lives. For robots to perform various supportive tasks and harmoniously coexist with people in human environments (e.g., homes, schools, and hospitals), they need to be designed and developed to interface with people in natural and intuitive ways. The field of Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) is highly interdisciplinary, incorporating methods and techniques from human-computer interaction, robotics, psychology, artificial intelligence, and other fields. Students in the class will present assigned research papers and lead discussions about cutting-edge peer-reviewed HRI publications. Throughout the term, students will form small groups of teams and will complete a course project that addresses an HRI-related research question of their choice.

CS 59300SER: Software Engineering/Robotics
Instructor: Kingston, Zachary
Days/Time: MWF 12:30p-1:20p
Location: LWSN B134
OK MS as elective: YES
OK PhD as 1 of 6: YES
Pre-requisites: UG students B or better in MA 26500 or MA 35100 and CS 25100 and CS 25200
Course description: Developing software for robot systems is challenging as they must sense, actuate, and represent the physical world. Sensing the physical world is usually noisy, actuating in and on the world is often inaccurate, and the knowledge and representation of the world is incomplete and uncertain. In this class we will explore software engineering approaches to cope with those challenges. You will learn to use domain-specific abstractions, architectures, libraries, and validation approaches and tools to safely perform robot activities like motion, navigation, perception, planning, and interaction. Moreover, you will learn more about performance engineering and modern hardware acceleration to take algorithms to the next level necessary for real-time robotic performance. The expectation is that this course will open up new career options in robotics for students.

CS 59300-MLT: Machine Learning Theory
Instructor: Hanneke, Steve
Days/Time: TTh 10:30a-11:45a
Location: LWSN 1106
OK MS as elective:
Yes
OK PhD as 1 of 6:
Yes
Pre-requisites: UG students, MA/STAT 41600 Probability (or equivalent), CS 38100 Introduction to the Analysis of Algorithms (or equivalent).
Course description: This course focuses on the theory of machine learning. The aim is to provide knowledge and technical training sufficient to enable students to engage in novel research in machine learning theory. We will start with the basics: discussing what kinds of accuracy guarantees are possible for learning algorithms, what classes of concepts are learnable, and how much training data is necessary and sufficient to achieve a given accuracy guarantee. We will cover the formal proofs of all results, including discussion of the thought process that might lead one to come up with such proofs. Further topics may include the theories of online learning, boosting, model selection, regularization, universal consistency, sample compression schemes, multiclass classification, active learning, contextual bandits, reinforcement learning, and others. The course will also introduce students to recent trends in machine learning theory, including many open problems currently ripe for progress. The material covered will make connections to related subjects, such as statistics and probability theory, combinatorics, algorithm design and analysis, game theory, and information theory.
CS 59300QIS/MA 58800: Mathematics For QIS
Instructor: Samperton, Eric
Days/Time: TTh 10:30a-11:45a
Location: MATH 215
OK MS as elective:
Yes
OK PhD as 1 of 6:
Yes
Course Description: This course will be an introduction to the theory underlying quantum computing and topological quantum computing. The course is designed to be self-contained. We will start with the basics of classical boolean and invertible logic and move to its quantum generalization in terms of gates and U(n) actions. The quantum mechanical background from physics will also be given and a deeper discussion of spins and their representation theoretic description is planned. Starting from this paradigmatic example, the course will move to aspects needed for topological quantum computing or field theoretical formulations. The last part of the course will be dedicated to additional topics.
