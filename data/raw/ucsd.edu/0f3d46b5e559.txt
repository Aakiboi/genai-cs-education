CSE-190 - Topics in Computer Science and Engineering (2026-2027)
Updated: November 6th, 2026
CSE-190 is a topics of special interest in Computer Science and Engineering course. Topics may vary from quarter to quarter.
Prerequisites also vary per course/per instructor. Department approval required.
Units: 4
CSE-190 is typically offered every quarter as staffing allows.
CSE-190 may be repeated for credit a maximum of 3 times (maximum of 12 units; assuming courses taken for a different topic)
A maximum of
one
CSE-190 may be enrolled/waitlisted per quarter
Note:
For the Fall 2023 Computer Science (CS26) curriculum, all CSE 190 courses will be labeled with a corresponding "Tag(s)" (Systems, Theory/Abstraction, and/or Applications of Computing). CSE-190 offerings before Fall 2023 are untagged but may be used as an Open CSE Elective for Computer Science majors who have changed to the FA23 CS26 curriculum.

Description:
The future of software engineering may lie in managing agents, which write and maintain code on a software engineer’s behalf. To obtain a software engineering position, it may no longer be enough to master the traditional basics; design skills will be essential. What constitutes a good software design? How can we analyze the tradeoffs of design candidates at a high level so that we can evaluate agents’ work and direct them to create software artifacts that will stand the test of time? This class will investigate these questions in the context of individual or small-group projects whose implementation is assisted by AI but whose design is managed by humans. Through reading, discussion, and iteration on artifacts, students will develop their design taste to enable them to make wise design choices. Students will be expected to provide their own access to an AI coding agent such as Claude Code for use in this class. In-person class attendance will be essential.

CSE-190 (
005-000-LE)
:
SWEET (Software Engineering Experiences and Training)
with
Adalbert Gerald Soosai Raj
Prerequisite:
CSE 110: Software Engineering and CSE 190: Working with Large Codebases.
CS Curriculum Tag(s)
:  Applications of Computing
To enroll:
Application will be posted here closer to the booking period.
Description:
SWEET teaches students what to build, why to build it, and how to know it worked. Students work in teams with community partners to build software that solves real problems that real people have. Using human centered design, teams interview users and stakeholders, define the problem, prototype ideas, and test them with the people they are meant to help. Teams then build, ship, and maintain their solutions using agentic software engineering. Students work with AI coding agents to plan, write, test, and review code. They stay fully responsible for the result. Every student must understand what their team built and why, and be able to explain it to different audiences, from community partners to technical managers in code walkthrough presentations. Along the way, students face the real difficulties of designing, building, and maintaining software that impacts people. These include unclear requirements, competing needs, trade offs, accessibility, privacy, ethics, and long-term maintenance. Students also practice teamwork, communicating with non-technical partners, and handing off software that a partner can keep using. Topics include user research, problem framing, prototyping and usability testing, agile practices, code review and testing, evaluating AI generated code, and measuring whether software actually meets user needs.

Description:
Generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools that synthesize rich output from user-supplied prompts have enabled new kinds of programming. This includes both AI systems that edit source code (AI as agents that assist with coding), and AI systems that use API calls to an LLM to provide user-facing features (AI as API). In this course, we explore both of these with hands-on use of these tools to build applications, along with some background about how generative AI works (and when it fails to work) in this setting.

CS Curriculum Tag(s)
: Systems
To enroll:
Submit a Triton Enrollment Authorization (TEA) request in the
Triton Student System

Description:
This course is about AI agent infrastructure. Students will learn the fundamentals about AI agent backend, including LLM inference, agent tooling, agent memory and context, agent sandboxes and hosting, edge computing for AI agents, agentic security and trustworthy, and agentic reinforcement learning. This course is not about AI/ML theory or agent frameworks/applications. It will focus on the backend of AI agents, with real use cases as the motivation of why backends are designed in certain ways. If you are looking for learning how to build AI agents and the theoretical foundations of AI agents, this is not the right course for you.

How the Web Tracks You

Undergraduate students must enroll in the undergraduate version, and graduate students must enroll in the graduate version. Students will not receive credit for both classes.

Description:
This course focuses on
web privacy,
covering a wide range of topics on both the “defensive” and “offensive” side of the field. In particular, we will focus on
web tracking —
the technical mechanisms that companies use to serve you hypertargeted advertisements or other personalized experiences. Students will learn the fundamentals of the web (i.e., the protocols and infrastructure that support the web today), how privacy is (or isn’t) protected today, and get hands-on experience (through PAs) implementing cookie-based tracking and inference, browser / device fingerprinting, and ad blocking technologies. The goal of the course is to introduce students to the vast tracking ecosystem that underlies the web and give students practical experience with these technologies as they are implemented in practice today. To complete the projects in this course, you will need to have (or gain) familiarity with all aspects of web programming, not limited to — web server deployment, JavaScript, web databases (e.g. SQL), client/server applications, and web extensions. We will not explicitly teach these in class and you will be expected to pick up what you need for each project on your own.

Description:
This
hands-on practicum
introduces students to understanding and working with large codebases, using both traditional software engineering practices and modern agentic AI tools. Students will learn how to fix bugs and modify/add features in existing, unfamiliar code, first building strong fundamentals in reading, navigating, and reasoning about unfamiliar systems, then extending those skills with agentic coding tools (e.g., Claude Code, GitHub Copilot) that can accelerate exploration, development, and testing in large codebases. Students will learn processes, techniques, and tools for working with large codebases, including how to critically evaluate and effectively direct AI-assisted workflows. Students will work in small teams on a large open-source codebase throughout the course. By the end of the course, students will develop practical skills and confidence to collaborate on complex software projects in real-world settings. This course is recommended for students
without
internship experience, although anyone is welcome to register.
