SWEN-262: Engineering of Software Subsystems
Syllabus
Course Description
An introduction to the principles of contemporary software design at
the subsystem and architecture level. Topics include reverse
engineering, refactoring, design of software subsystems, design
patterns, and a reinforcement of core software development lifecycle
skills including agile development, version control, UML, unit
testing, and more. Students will have an opportunity to practice
their software design and development skills on mid-to-large scale
software systems both individually, and as part of a team. AI use is
deliberately scoped throughout — restricted where it would substitute
for a student's own understanding, and structured with explicit
guardrails where it's used to support design and implementation work.
AI Policy
AI use in this course is deliberately scoped based on the specific activity and
the student's expected level of proficiency in that skillset.
Coding and implementation.
Students in SWEN-262 are expected to
be proficient object-oriented programmers after having learned and practiced OOP
in GCIS-123, GCIS-124, and SWEN-261. Whereas previous courses restricted the use
of AI to assist while you were learning the fundamentals of OOP, now that you
have mastered those fundamentals, you will often be
expected and
encouraged
to use AI assistance when writing code.
Design, analysis, and modeling.
Many of the other skills used in
this class — software design, state diagrams, design principles, design
patterns, and so on — are much less developed. The AI policy for tasks involving
those nascent and developing skills will be
much more prescribed and
restricted
.
Please pay close attention to the instructions on each activity indicating the
acceptable use policy for AI. Unless otherwise stated,
AI use is
prohibited
for any given activity.
Use of AI on the midterm or final exams constitutes a violation of academic
honesty.
Academic Honesty Policy
In SWEN-262, all submitted individual work (quizzes, exams, mini-designs, etc.)
is expected to be the result of your own, individual effort. Unless otherwise
indicated, use of AI tools is strictly prohibited.
All submitted team work (Design Project Release 1 & 2) is expected to be the
result of a collaborative effort from the entire team. Contributions to team
deliverables are expected to be the result of roughly equal effort across
members of the team. Unless otherwise specified, use of AI tools is strictly
prohibited. If one student on the team submits work that violates this policy,
the entire team shares responsibility
.
The penalty for a first minor offense of the course academic honesty policy is a
zero on the corresponding assignment. The penalty for a second offense or an
egregious offense is an F in the course. Please consult
RIT's
Student Academic Integrity Policy
for more information.

Course Objectives
This course treats software design as an engineering activity, not just a
programming activity — one where systems are analyzed, planned, and evolved
deliberately, not assembled ad hoc. By the end of the course, you will be able to:
Safely reverse-engineer and refactor an unfamiliar legacy codebase, using structured analysis and AI-assisted tooling to identify design problems before changing any code.
Individually design a software subsystem by applying an appropriate classic design pattern, and articulate that choice and its impact on the design using established design-principles language.
Work as part of an agile software development team to architect, design, and implement a mid-sized software system from a clean sheet through full implementation.
Learing Outcomes and Exam Study Guides
Method of Instruction
Flipped-classroom approach for some individualized learning, e.g., classic design patterns.
Individual work on reverse engineering, refactoring, and each release's mini-design.
Team work for the design project's design and implementation phases.
Some class time used for lectures, but the majority is for in-class work (both individuals and teams).
Students will also have an opportunity to present their individual work to the class, as well as presenting as part of their design project team.

Attendance Policy
Attending class is important because there will be time devoted to activities in almost
every class session that contribute to your grade. During the individual phases of the
course — the Reverse Engineering & Refactoring unit and the individual mini-design days
of each Design Project release — class time is largely used for your own independent work,
with the instructor and TA circulating to answer questions and give feedback in real time.
Once teams are formed, most classes will also include team meeting time, some of which will
be with the instructor discussing project work with each team. During these meetings with
the instructor, you will be able to ask questions about the project and receive early
feedback on your project work.
